Could Protons Program a Low-Loss Optical Weight on Silicon Nitride?

Version 0.4 — a decomposed hypothesis and experiment-prioritization case study

This document is not a feasibility claim. It breaks one untested photonic-memory hypothesis into the links that would all have to hold, ties each link to its nearest published evidence, and ranks the experiments that could falsify it, cheapest first.

Work in progress. This is a pre-experimental decomposition of an untested hypothesis, not a device proposal and not a claim that the device would work. It reports no device data. Every number on this page is a published literature value, never a result of this work. Most of the cited evidence concerns lithium rather than protons, visible rather than 1550 nm light, electrostatic rather than electrochemical doping, or calculation rather than measurement; each use is labelled. This page has not yet had a human archive pass.

Claim Block

The single claim that, if false, makes this work worthless: there is a range of proton doping in which a monolayer transition-metal dichalcogenide changes its refractive index near 1550 nm while adding little absorption, and the doping can be both reversed and held at open circuit.

Who can check it: no literature source located answers it; only experiment can. This page breaks it into six links (Section 3) and states which is cheapest to test first.

1. Abstract

WIP · draft 3 · pending human archive pass

Storing neural-network weights directly in the optical layer of a photonic accelerator would remove the repeated weight fetch from electronic memory. Non-volatile photonic weights have been demonstrated on integrated waveguides using phase-change materials, ferroelectric barium titanateV1, and copper-ion ferroionic two-dimensional heterostructures, one of which reports 8-bit storage with projected retention beyond ten yearsV2. Separately, protons have been used to switch optical properties outside integrated photonics, in a thin-film proton pumpV3 and in room-temperature protonation of vanadium dioxideV4. In a targeted search we did not find a waveguide-integrated optical weight programmed by protons; the search scope is stated in Section 7, and absence from it is not proof of absence.

We ask whether such a weight could exist, and decompose the question into six links that would all have to hold: a low-loss index response to proton doping, room-temperature reversibility, sufficient doping density, open-circuit retention, a sealed proton source, and no added background loss. For each link we give the nearest published evidence and its limits. None of the links has been demonstrated for protons in this configuration. The nearest evidence is mixed. Electrostatic gating of monolayer WS2 on silicon nitride has produced |Δn/Δk| ≈ 125V7, and hydrogen plasma treatment can n-dope monolayer MoS2V8. Against this, heavy electron doping by intercalated lithium drives MoS2 toward a metallic phaseV10, and a calculation suggests that hydrogen bound in monolayer MoS2 has migration barriers above 1 eVV13, which, if borne out, would favour retention but work against room-temperature reversal.

The first link is also the cheapest to test, on a flat substrate without a waveguide, and is bounded on both sides by known failure modes. We therefore present the work as a ladder of falsifiable steps rather than a design. Because copper-ion devices already combine low loss, multilevel storage and long projected retention, a proton weight would be worth building only if it offered a distinct advantage, most plausibly front-end fabrication compatibility; that advantage is itself an assumption to be tested.

2. How this document is built

WIP

The document follows one sequence, applied to a single hypothesis:

  1. State the hypothesis as the claim that, if false, makes the work worthless (the Claim Block).
  2. Break it into links that must all hold (Section 3).
  3. For each link, record the nearest published evidence and how that evidence differs from the case in question: species of ion, doping mechanism, wavelength, and whether it was measured or calculated.
  4. Record where links pull against each other (Section 4).
  5. Choose the cheapest experiment that could falsify the most links at once (Section 5).
  6. Resolve every reference to its title and claimed authors before it is cited (Section 6), and keep withdrawn or narrowed claims visible rather than deleting them (Section 7).

This sequence is not offered as a new method. It draws on established practices such as pre-registration, kill criteria set before data, and structured evidence arguments. Its use here is a case study, and the proton hypothesis is the subject of that case study rather than a result.

Provenance. The literature search and drafting were AI-assisted. Most tombstones (T2, T4, T5, T6) record claims made during that drafting which were later narrowed or withdrawn when references were checked against publisher and repository records. They are kept because they show where unchecked claims entered and how they were caught.

Review status. One external AI review has assessed the framing of an earlier version. No independent review has yet tested the physics of the six links, the completeness of the search, or the evidence assigned to each link. Agreement with the structure is not evidence that the links are correctly stated.

3. The six links

WIP · all six are open

Each link is stated as what would have to be true, followed by the nearest evidence found and how that evidence differs from the case in question. Kill thresholds are to be set from the literature before any laboratory work, not after data arrive.

L1 · Low-loss index response open

Must be trueProton doping of a TMD monolayer shifts its refractive index near 1550 nm with |Δn/Δk| well above 10.

Nearest evidenceElectrostatic gating of WS2 on SiN gave |Δn/Δk| ≈ 125 in the near infraredV7. In MoS2, gated absorption stayed constant away from the exciton bandsV9. Hydrogen plasma n-dopes monolayer MoS2V8.

How it differsV7 and V9 are electrostatic, not proton doping; V9 was measured in the visible. V8 was observed through photoluminescence, not index or absorption. No measurement of proton-doped TMD optical constants near 1550 nm was found.

L2 · Room-temperature reversibility open

Must be trueProtons can be electrochemically added to and removed from the monolayer at room temperature.

Nearest evidenceHydrogen doping of MoS2 was reversed, but by a chlorine plasma, not electrochemicallyV8. A calculation finds hydrogen in monolayer MoS2 stable as an interstitial or H–S adatom, with migration barriers above 1 eVV13.

How it differsV13 is a calculation for MoS2 grown with incidental hydrogen; it does not model electrochemical removal. See tension A.

L3 · Sufficient doping below the phase-change ceiling open

Must be trueA thin solid proton reservoir delivers enough carriers for a useful phase shift without driving the monolayer into a metallic phase.

Nearest evidenceLithium intercalation drives bulk MoS2 from the semiconducting 2H to the metallic 1T structureV10. Neighbouring layers change the thresholdV11V12.

How it differsAll ceiling evidence is for lithium, mostly in bulk or few-layer material. Neither the carrier density needed for a useful phase shift nor the proton ceiling for a monolayer has been established here; both are desk-stage estimates still to be made.

L4 · Open-circuit retention open

Must be trueThe doped state holds without applied bias, next to an optical mode, for a useful time.

Nearest evidenceSingle-layer hBN as a proton-sieving electrolyte was reported to improve retention in proton electrochemical memoryV6. A proton pump retained an optical state at open circuitV3.

How it differsV6 is an electronic device with a different geometry; V3 switches a metal film, not a TMD. Neither addresses a monolayer on a waveguide.

L5 · Sealed proton source open

Must be trueProtons come from a sealed reservoir rather than ambient humidity.

Nearest evidenceBoth room-temperature proton-optics results in this ledger draw on water: the proton pump on ambient moistureV3, the VO2 device on a water-infiltrated glass gateV4.

How it differsNo sealed proton source for optical switching is in this ledger.

L6 · No added background loss open

Must be trueMobile hydrogen near the mode does not add significant absorption near 1550 nm through bonded-hydrogen overtones or defects.

Nearest evidenceNone gated in this ledger yet. Sources on hydrogen-related absorption in silicon nitride and oxides near 1.5 µm must pass the DOI gate before this link can cite them.

How it differsNot applicable until evidence is sourced.

4. Stated tensions

WIP

A. Retention against reversibility. If the calculated barriers of V13 apply to the device, hydrogen bound strongly enough to give retention (L4) may be too strongly bound to remove at room temperature (L2). If it leaves easily, it may not stay. The flat-substrate test must therefore measure reversal, not doping alone.

B. Stack order. A calculation suggests an hBN interface thermodynamically favours the metallic phase of lithium-intercalated MoS2, while the same authors hypothesise a counteracting barrier from the interface itselfV11. In a related lithium experiment, graphene–MoS2 interfaces needed a 0.8 V larger potential than hBN–MoS2 interfaces to nucleate the metallic phaseV12. Whether this carries over to protons is unknown, but it suggests the layer against the TMD is a design choice with consequences for L3, not a neutral one.

C. The competing route. Copper-ion devices already deliver several of these linksV2. A proton route that only matches them adds nothing.

5. The cheapest first test

WIP · design not yet drafted

A TMD monolayer on a flat substrate, stepped through increasing proton loading and then back, with near-infrared reflectance or ellipsometry at each step. It addresses L1 and L2 together and probes the lower edge of L3. It needs no waveguide, sealing or retention measurement. If no window exists in which the index moves with little absorption and the change reverses, the remaining links need not be tested.

7. Tombstones and search scope

Claims made during drafting that were withdrawn or narrowed. They stay visible rather than being silently corrected.

T1 · narrowed

A 2026 review characterizes near-infrared electrochemical modulation in general as amplitude-dominant. The source sentence describes one device class. Replaced by the host-specific claim in V5.

T2 · withdrawn

No one has combined ionic storage with a low-loss 2D optical layer on a waveguide. Falsified by V2. The surviving gap is protons specifically.

T3 · excluded

Monolayer WS2 shows Δn = 0.53 with Δk = 0.004. Seen only in a secondary review. Only the primary abstract value is used.

T4 · withdrawn

Copper-ion ferroionic weights write slowly, so protons would win on write speed. The slow shift belongs to one lateral-migration device; write speed is no longer claimed as the proton advantage.

T5 · narrowed

An hBN interface makes the lossy phase transition easier. That was the calculation only. The same authors hypothesise a counteracting barrier (V11), and the experimental comparison (V12) is relative to graphene and for lithium. Restated as tension B.

T6 · withdrawn

Hydrogen impurities are proposed as an origin of unintentional doping in TMDs (citing a 2017 Nanoscale paper). That paper places the hydrogen in the underlying SiO2, not the TMD, and its authors were not resolved. Replaced by V13.

T7 · excluded

A 2025 paper on electrochemical proton intercalation stabilising metallic 1T MoS2 for catalysis. Its authors could not be resolved, so it is not used. V10 covers the phase-transition point.

Search scope. Searches were run within AI-assisted sessions using general web search: English-language sources plus targeted regional passes on Korea, Japan, China and Europe, and a targeted pass on hydrogen and proton doping of TMDs. Patent databases and non-English full texts were not searched systematically. The gap claim holds only within this scope.
